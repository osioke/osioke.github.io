projects

  • LORE (2026–present): an organisational knowledge-transfer platform — capturing what an experienced person actually knows, from what they do rather than how they'd explain it, and turning it into training for whoever replaces them. Built and tested with real users on Firebase and vanilla JavaScript. An early version paused after user research showed the HR workflow I'd assumed didn't match how these teams actually work; now back in active development, with a proper diagnosis of what the extraction pipeline was missing.
  • Growth Clinic & Community Staples (2019–2025): a talent incubator and its sister publication, both built on the same idea — that communities can be deliberately engineered to turn ordinary people into skilled ones. Wound down at the end of 2025. The model had drifted from what actually worked at Devcenter (placing people in real paid work) toward selling training directly to individuals, and I can trace exactly where that started.
  • Shop (2025): a full-stack inventory and sales-management system I built for my mum's convenience store — role-based access, real-time inventory, credit tracking, sales reporting. She hasn't ended up using it day to day, but it's a solid recent proof that I can take AI-assisted building from idea to something a real business could run on.
  • Frintern (2018): a web app matching people to tech skills they're naturally good at, and helping them learn those skills. In a 2018 pilot, participants learned a skill and applied it in 2–3 hours, down from a 20-hour baseline. Now sunsetted — I moved on to full-time roles without the technical ability I have now to keep building it myself, back before AI-assisted coding existed, and left it in my co-founder's hands.

A few more — including Disease Info, a gameshow app built for a streamer, a self-improvement RPG, and a manga tracker I use myself — plus my full work history, live on the /work page.

outside of work

Most of what I like outside work comes down to the same thing: figuring out how something works by getting inside it. I never did well in a classroom — structured, one-way learning didn't click for me — so I found my own way in, by working with and helping people, which turned out to teach me just as much about myself. That's probably the earliest form of "community" I ever took part in, before I had a word for it.

That same curiosity is why I like chess (analysis with a board), games and digital worlds (systems with room to explore inside them), and film, cartoons, and web comics (worlds bigger than the one I'm standing in). For a while I documented what I found — street photography, a digital museum called Gani — but these days I'm more reserved about sharing publicly; it's gotten harder to reach the specific people I'd actually want to connect with through it, from where I am now.
